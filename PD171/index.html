Development of the UC4AFC Monosyllabic Word List
Greg A. O'Beirne, Jayne Newbury, Gemma Anderson, Eliza Riley, Hanson Chong, Sarah Murray, Toby Macrae  ·  University of Canterbury, New Zealand
View
Demo PDF ↗
Abstract
›

Auditory processing disorder (APD) is characterized by a variety of symptoms that can present as listening difficulties despite normal peripheral hearing. The University of Canterbury Adaptive Speech test – Filtered Words (UCAST-FW; O'Beirne et al., 2012; Rickard et al., 2013) is an adaptive low-pass filtered speech test for use in APD assessment. However, it originally used North American stimuli (the NU-CHIPS) which are less suitable for New Zealand and Australian children.

To address this, we have created the UC4AFC monosyllabic word list using phonemic contrasts more appropriate for New Zealand and Australian varieties of English. We developed an initial list of 98 target words, each with three phonemic, semantic, or unrelated foils, recorded in audio and video formats. Four candidate images per item were evaluated by adults (n=43); spontaneous naming was assessed in 4-year-olds (n=30); and receptive picture-pointing was tested in 4-year-olds (n=45). Combining paediatric data with adult psychometric data (n=35) for low-pass-filtered stimuli, we selected a final word list of 66 items across two balanced lists. The UC4AFC provides robust four-alternative monosyllabic stimuli more suitable for NZ and Australian children, enabling more accurate paediatric evaluation of speech discrimination and APD.

Auditory processing disorder (APD) is characterized by a variety of symptoms that can present as listening difficulties despite normal peripheral hearing. The University of Canterbury Adaptive Speech test – Filtered Words (UCAST-FW; O'Beirne et al., 2012; Rickard et al., 2013) is an adaptive low-pass filtered speech test for use in APD assessment. However, it originally used North American stimuli (the NU-CHIPS) which are less suitable for New Zealand and Australian children.

To address this, we created the University of Canterbury Four-Alternative Forced Choice (UC4AFC) monosyllabic word list using phonemic contrasts more appropriate for New Zealand and Australian varieties of English. The initial 98-word list (created by Murray and O'Beirne; Murray, 2012) consisted of target words, two phonemic foils, and a third foil that was either semantically-related or phonemically-unrelated to the target. Here we summarise a series of recent refinement and validation studies to enable the clinical paediatric use of the UC4AFC word list, including in the UCAST-FW.

Study 1 Recording Words & Selecting Images (Anderson, 2024)
›
Objectives
  • Select representative images for each of the 98 target words
  • Create audiovisual recordings for future AV-integration studies
  • Evaluate low-pass-filter intelligibility characteristics of the recordings

The study had three objectives: (i) to select four candidate images for each word and identify the most representative image for each UC4AFC target word; (ii) to create audiovisual recordings of the UC4AFC wordlist to allow for future auditory-visual integration studies; and (iii) to determine intelligibility functions for the recorded words under low-pass filtering conditions.

Method
  • 392 candidate images collected (4 per target word), from freely available licensable sources
  • 43 adults selected the "best image for children <12 years" via online survey
  • Audiovisual recordings created for all target words
  • 35 adults completed auditory-alone identification under 8–9 low-pass filter conditions (200–1500 Hz cut-off)
  • Responses collected in:
    • Open-set format (participant repeated the word)
    • Closed-set 4AFC format (participant selected from four alternatives)
  • Data used to evaluate low-pass-filter psychometric characteristics for word list normalisation

A total of 392 candidate images (4 images × 98 target words) were obtained from freely available licensable sources. Forty-three adults completed an online survey in which they selected the image they thought best represented each target word for children under 12 years of age.

Audiovisual recordings were created for all target words. Following a 15-item practice session, 35 adults identified the recorded words presented auditory-alone under 8–9 low-pass filter conditions (200–1500 Hz cut-off frequencies). Responses were collected in both open-set format (participant repeated the word) and closed-set format (participant selected from four alternatives). These data were used to evaluate low-pass-filter psychometric characteristics of the word list.

Results
  • Strong image agreement observed for several target words:
    • chalk (98%), comb (95%), cap (86%), dirt (81%), leg (81%)
  • Low-pass-filter results informed later item selection for the finalised lists

Strong image agreement was observed for several target words, including: chalk (98%), comb (95%), cap (86%), dirt (81%), and leg (81%). These findings supported selection of final image stimuli for subsequent validation studies. Low-pass-filter results were subsequently used to inform item selection for the finalised word lists.

Study 2 Spontaneous Naming by 4-Year-Olds (Chong, 2025)
›
Objectives
  • Evaluate whether four-year-olds consistently produced the intended labels for UC4AFC images
  • Quantify variability in naming responses and naming latency

To evaluate whether four-year-old children consistently produced the intended labels for UC4AFC images, and to quantify variability in naming responses.

Method
  • 30 four-year-old children from low socioeconomic backgrounds
  • Children viewed 98 selected UC4AFC images + 17 alternative candidate images, in random order
  • Images preceded by audio prompts ("This is…", "These are…", "This person is…", etc.)
  • A 1 kHz beep synchronised with image presentation (SE = 7.4 ms) enabled latency measurement
  • Analyses included:
    • Intended name agreement — proportion producing the target label
    • Target word inclusion — target word contained within a longer response
    • Naming diversity — entropy statistic H (Snodgrass & Vanderwart, 1980), expressed as % of theoretical maximum

      H = k Σ i = 1 pi log2(1pi)

      where k is the number of unique names assigned to an image and pi is the proportion of participants providing each name. H values are expressed as percentages of the theoretical maximum entropy (log₂ 30 = 4.91).

Thirty four-year-old children attending a preschool in a low socioeconomic area participated. Children were shown the 98 adult-selected images from Anderson (2024), along with 17 alternative candidate images. Images were presented in random order and preceded by audio prompts such as 'This is', 'This is a', 'These are', 'This person likes to', and 'This person is'. A 1 kHz beep occurred near-simultaneously with image presentation (SE = 7.4 ms), enabling measurement of naming latency from the session audio recordings.

Intended name agreement was calculated as the proportion of children producing the target label for each image. Target word inclusion was additionally calculated when the target word was contained within a longer response. Naming variability was quantified using the information statistic H (Snodgrass & Vanderwart, 1980):

H = k Σ i = 1 pi log2(1pi)

where k is the number of unique names assigned to an image and pi is the proportion of participants providing each name. H values are expressed as percentages of the theoretical maximum entropy (log₂ 30 = 4.91).

Results
  • Naming diversity ranged from near 0% (high agreement, low variability) to ~90% of theoretical maximum entropy (low agreement, high variability)
  • Strong positive relationship between naming diversity and intended name agreement for exact matches (R² = 0.56)
  • No such relationship for target word inclusion (R² = 0.00)

Normalised naming diversity ranged from near 0% (high intended name agreement and low naming variability) to approximately 90%, reflecting greater diversity of naming responses (Bartos et al., 2022). There was a strong positive relationship between normalised naming diversity and intended name agreement for exact name matches (R² = 0.56), but no such relationship for target word inclusion (R² = 0.00).

Study 3 Receptive Identification by 4-Year-Olds (Riley, 2026)
›
Objective
  • Determine whether four-year-olds could accurately and rapidly identify intended targets in 4AFC format

To evaluate whether 4-year-old children could accurately and rapidly identify the intended target items in the UC4AFC four-alternative forced-choice format.

Method
  • 45 four-year-old children from low-to-mid socioeconomic backgrounds
  • Completed receptive 4AFC task using all 98 UC4AFC image sets
  • Children selected the image corresponding to each recorded target word
  • Both identification accuracy and response latency recorded for all 98 items

Forty-five four-year-old children from low-to-mid socioeconomic backgrounds completed a receptive four-alternative forced-choice task using the 98 UC4AFC image sets. Children selected the image corresponding to each recorded target word, enabling measurement of identification accuracy and response latency.

Results
  • ~75% of items achieved both ≥75% identification accuracy and median latency <3.5 s
  • These items identified as strong candidates for final inclusion
  • Poorer-performing items considered for exclusion

Approximately three quarters of the items demonstrated both high identification accuracy (>75%) and short response latencies (<3.5 s). These items were identified as strong candidates for inclusion in the finalised UC4AFC word lists, while poorer-performing items were considered for exclusion.

Final List Final List Composition

  • Final inclusion decisions incorporated:
    • Low-pass filter normalisation results (Anderson, 2024)
    • Expressive naming results (Chong, 2025)
    • Receptive identification results (Riley, 2026)
    • Phonemic foil balance requirements
  • No included target words fell below 75% correct on receptive testing
  • Only chin and ship exceeded 3.5 s median receptive latency
  • Poor spontaneous naming did not necessarily predict poor receptive identification:
    • Ten included items had <25% expressive target-word inclusion (night, nurse, coat, knees, boot, ship, seed, lick, cot, head)
    • These items still achieved 85% ± 5% receptive accuracy
  • Expressive and receptive latencies showed weak but significant correlation (r = .35, p < .01)

The final inclusion/exclusion decisions factored in the low-pass-filter normalisation results of Anderson (2024), the expressive results of Chong (2025), the receptive results of Riley (2026), and essential phonemic foil requirements.

Of the target words included, none fell below 75% correct on the four-year-old receptive task, and only two items (chin and ship) had median latencies over 3.5 s. The ten included words that had lower than 25% target word inclusion on the four-year-old expressive task (night, nurse, coat, knees, boot, ship, seed, lick, cot, and head) scored 85% ± 5% correct on the receptive task, indicating that poorer spontaneous naming performance did not necessarily predict poorer receptive identification performance. The expressive and receptive median latencies were significantly but weakly correlated (r = .35, p < .01).

Conclusion Summary & Implications
›
  • The UC4AFC provides robust four-alternative monosyllabic stimuli more suitable for NZ and Australian children than previous North American materials
  • These findings support its use in paediatric speech discrimination assessment and future APD evaluation

The UC4AFC provides robust four-alternative monosyllabic stimuli that are more suitable for New Zealand and Australian children than previous North American materials. These findings support its use in paediatric speech discrimination assessment and future APD evaluation.
